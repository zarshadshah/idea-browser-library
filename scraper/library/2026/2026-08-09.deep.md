Short drama apps pulled in $2.8B in 2024, double the year before. Romance and revenge serials play to millions on apps like ReelShort. Writers with real audiences on Wattpad see the demand, then hit the wall: pitch decks, studio contacts, contracts that sign away story rights. Supply stays locked behind studio gates while the audience asks for more.

Storysynth lets a writer paste a finished script, generate episodes with AI actors, and publish to a catalog with a per-episode paywall. Video generation renders the performances and backgrounds, and the writer sets characters, pricing, and a preview before publishing. ReelShort produces its own serials and owns the IP, leaving the independent writer as hired talent. Viewers pay a few dollars an episode, and the writer keeps the majority and the rights. One script becomes a sellable series, live the same week it is finished.

Wire a script-to-video pipeline on Runway or HeyGen, connect Stripe, and stand up a catalog where viewers unlock episodes. Recruit ten romance writers who bring an audience from Wattpad, and have each drop a five-episode series at a price they choose. Hardest part is render quality: AI video has to hold a viewer through the episode they paid for, or completion craters. Track conversions and completion, then iterate on render speed, quality, and checkout. Romance and revenge only, so the early catalog stays coherent.

Revenue runs on two lines: a $30 monthly subscription for unlimited conversions, and a platform fee on each viewer purchase tied to how well a series sells. One-off $9.99 trials run a script through the full pipeline before a writer subscribes. Writer subreddits, Facebook drama groups, and YouTube workflow breakdowns carry the pitch to people chasing the outcome. Four short-drama apps hit the US top 50 last year, proof the format works at scale. Creator tooling for the category stays wide open.
