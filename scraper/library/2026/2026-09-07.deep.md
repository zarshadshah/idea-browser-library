6.5
/10

THE IDEA

A working film composer opens a Kontakt library called Ethno World 6, loads the duduk, and hears something that will not sit in a real cue: no round robins, weak legato, one dynamic. So they buy a second library, then a third. On VI-Control they trade prices ($240 for a Sonica Koto, $119 for a Premier Sound Factory Koto, $799 for Impact Soundworks' Complete World Bundle, down from $1,612) and complain that older libraries from ten years ago sound better than new ones because someone took 'Extra Care' per sample. Meanwhile Splice bought Spitfire Audio for $50M in April 2025 and in October launched Splice INSTRUMENT at $12.99/mo, bundling every Spitfire Originals sound. AI floods streaming (Deezer says 20K AI tracks upload daily, 18% of all uploads), and working composers reach for sounds no model can fake.

The idea is a boutique sample label that records one rare instrument at a time, deeply, in Kontakt. Mobile team, real player, real room, every articulation and round robin. One instrument every six to eight weeks, sold direct at $79 to $199, bundled at $499 to $799 after a dozen ship. Not a royalty split to the instrument owner (the market runs on flat royalty-free licensing, so the owner gets a flat session fee, sometimes with sync backend). A category-creation play in a niche where search is dead but the watering holes are loud.

WHY NOW

Splice paid $50M for Spitfire in April 2025, then bundled it into a $13/mo plugin in October

Two things broke open in 2025 that were not true in 2024. First, Splice acquired Spitfire Audio in April 2025 for a reported $50 million and, in October, launched Splice INSTRUMENT: a $12.99 per month plugin that bundles the full Spitfire Originals collection plus 1,200 multi-sampled presets. Spitfire's free LABS series was folded in and shut down as a standalone. The 'common ground' sample library, the pianos, strings, and pads that boutique developers used to charge $79 to $249 for, is now a Netflix subscription with 10 million producers already on the platform. A small developer cannot beat that price. What Splice does not have, and cannot cheaply record, is the long tail: a specific hurdy gurdy from a specific maker, a Bulgarian duduk played by the person who knows it, a museum piece with one working copy in the world.

Second, AI music crossed a tipping point. Deezer disclosed that 20,000 AI-generated tracks were uploaded to its platform every day in 2025, or 18% of all uploads. SlopTracker put the Spotify figure at 28%. Suno reached about 100 million users and raised at a $2.45 billion valuation. Universal and Warner signed AI licensing deals with Suno and Udio in October and November 2025. Working film, TV, game, and library composers now compete against a wave of generic AI cues. Their defense is the opposite move: authenticated, human-recorded, one-of-a-kind sounds that no model was trained on and no $13 subscription owns.
