Totaled Car Payout Saver (catch lowball payouts)
7.3
/10

THE IDEA

Your car just got totaled. The insurer sends a CCC ONE valuation report with a number that will not buy the same car back. One driver got $9,200 for a vehicle that costs $13,500 to replace at the same year and mileage: a $4,300 hole through no fault of his own. This is not a rare complaint. Total-loss frequency hit a record 23.1% of all auto claims in 2025, roughly 3.5 million cars a year, and a federal jury already ruled State Farm's software systematically underpaid 37,000 plaintiffs. Progressive paid $91 million and State Farm paid $15.6 million in 2025 and 2026 for the exact line-items inside those reports.

The product reads the insurer's PDF, pulls live listings from Autotrader, Cars.com, and CarGurus for the same year, trim, and mileage in the driver's zip, and returns a one-page verdict on the dollar gap in minutes. $49 for the full report with comps and a rebuttal template. If the gap is big enough to justify a fight, the tool hands the case to a licensed partner appraiser who signs a USPAP report and files it under the Appraisal Clause for $299. The founder keeps a $100 referral. Software does the intake work an appraiser currently does by hand, which is why the partner can undercut the $399 solo shops.

The demand side is right there. CCC's own Q2 2025 Crash Course reports total-loss frequency at 22.6% of all auto claims, up from prior years, and more than 70% of those totaled cars are seven years or older, exactly the vehicles with wide price spreads and weak comps. Roughly 4 million total-loss claims flow through U.S. insurers each year. Every settlement headline ("State Farm to pay $335 per driver") teaches the next claimant that the first offer is negotiable. Add YouTube negotiation videos with 100K+ views, active Reddit threads, and a growing bench of appraisers publicly citing Chadwick and Volino in dispute letters. WHY NOW

Progressive paid $91M and State Farm paid $15.6M in 2025-2026 for undervaluing totaled cars

The timing is legal, not technical. In 2025 and 2026 federal courts stopped treating "the software says so" as a defense. Progressive paid $48 million to 93,000 New York drivers in the Volino case, then another $43 million to Georgia drivers in Brown, both over Mitchell's Projected Sold Adjustment. A federal jury sided with State Farm policyholders in Arkansas in June 2025 over CCC ONE's Typical Negotiation Adjustment, and the $15.6 million settlement got final approval in March 2026. In October 2025 the Sixth Circuit upheld class certification in Clippinger v. State Farm, breaking with five other circuits and opening the door for new state-by-state cases. A fresh federal case against State Farm in Alabama landed in December 2025. Court filings pegged the average Progressive underpayment at 6.5% per claim. The insurer playbook ("trust the algorithm") is no longer a safe answer to a policyholder or a judge.

The demand side is right there. CCC's own Q2 2025 Crash Course reports total-loss frequency at 22.6% of all auto claims, up from prior years, and more than 70% of those totaled cars are seven years or older, exactly the vehicles with wide price spreads and weak comps. Roughly 4 million total-loss claims flow through U.S. insurers each year. Every settlement headline ("State Farm to pay $335 per driver") teaches the next claimant that the first offer is negotiable. Add YouTube negotiation videos with 100K+ views, active Reddit threads, and a growing bench of appraisers publicly citing Chadwick and Volino in dispute letters. The customer now arrives at your product already suspicious, already Googling, and already told by a court that they are probably right.
